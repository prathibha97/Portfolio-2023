Most writing about service boundaries is written from first principles — bounded contexts, the single-responsibility principle, a diagram with tidy boxes. I want to write the version from the other side, where the boxes were already drawn by a regulator and my job was to find out which of my instincts survived contact with them.

The platform does financial compliance: KYC, AML, fraud scoring, transaction monitoring. Seventeen Go services. Kafka between them. Kubernetes underneath, provisioned through Terraform.

## Why compliance is different

In most products you get to choose your boundaries and the cost of a bad one is refactoring. Here two constraints arrive before you write anything.

The first is that **the audit trail is a product requirement, not an implementation detail**. A regulator can ask why a transaction was flagged in March and the answer has to be reconstructible — which inputs, which rule version, which decision, in order. That pushes you toward durable event logs early, and it means a service that cannot explain itself is not finished.

The second is that **some boundaries are legal, not technical**. Which data can sit beside which other data is sometimes a jurisdiction question. When that is true, no amount of "but it would be simpler as one service" wins the argument.

## What Kafka changed

Kafka is usually sold as decoupling. In practice it moves the coupling rather than removing it: services stop depending on each other's availability and start depending on each other's **event schemas**, which is a better trade but not a free one.

The useful consequence for boundary-drawing is that the question changes. It stops being *who calls whom* and becomes *who owns this fact*. A service that emits an event is claiming to be the authority on something. If two services emit events about the same fact, you have found a boundary error, and you found it on a whiteboard instead of in production.

## Seventeen is a number, not an achievement

I want to be careful here, because "we run seventeen microservices" is the kind of sentence that sounds like a flex and usually signals a distributed monolith.

The test I use: **can this service be deployed on its own, and can it be understood on its own?** If shipping a change means coordinating two repositories, those are one service wearing two names. If understanding why a number is wrong means reading four services, the boundary is in the wrong place regardless of what the deployment topology says.
